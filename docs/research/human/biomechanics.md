# Human Biomechanics — General Principles

Companion to [../basketball/biomechanics.md](../basketball/biomechanics.md) (which
covers basketball-specific elite patterns). This file covers the general human
movement science those patterns are instances of.

## Newton's laws, applied to a moving body

1. **Inertia:** a body in motion stays in motion — direction changes need an
   external force (the floor, via friction under the foot). This is why every
   turn requires a planted foot pushing sideways; you cannot turn "in the air"
   without prior angular momentum.
2. **F = ma:** acceleration is proportional to force and inversely to mass. A
   heavier or longer-limbed body accelerates its limbs more slowly for the same
   muscular force — part of why big players "look slower" even at equal foot
   speed.
3. **Action-reaction:** pushing off the ground produces an equal, opposite
   ground reaction force — this is the entire mechanism of walking, running,
   and jumping. No motion without the floor pushing back.

## The stretch-shortening cycle

Muscle-tendon units store elastic energy when stretched rapidly (eccentric
loading) then release it in the following contraction (concentric) — this is
why a countermovement (dip) before a jump produces more force than a jump from
a static crouch. The effect requires the transition from stretch to shortening
to happen fast (~200ms window) — a slow dip loses the stored energy to heat.

## Kinetic chain principle

Power generated by large proximal muscles (hips, trunk) transfers outward
through the chain to distal segments (hands, feet), gaining speed at each link
(same principle as a whip). This is why shooting power comes from the legs, not
the arm — the arm is the last, fastest-but-weakest link, positioned to aim, not
generate force.

## Degrees of freedom and redundancy

The human body has many more joints than strictly needed for any single task —
the same hand position can be reached by infinitely many joint-angle
combinations. The nervous system resolves this by minimizing effort/jerk
("optimal control" models) — which is why natural motion looks smooth and
economical, and why animation that ignores this (moving every joint maximally,
independently) looks robotic.

## Symmetry and asymmetry

Bilateral symmetry governs stance and static poses; nearly all dynamic motion
is asymmetric (one side leads) — walking, throwing, shooting. Perfectly
symmetric dynamic poses (both arms doing the exact mirrored thing at the exact
same time) are rare in nature and read as artificial.

## Game application (Cyber Hoops)

- Stretch-shortening cycle = the pre-load dip already specced (arcade-
  compressed to 50–70ms, inside that 200ms elastic window — physically valid,
  not just a stylistic choice).
- Kinetic chain = why AnimationController's pelvis-leads-shoulders-lag ordering
  (migration 4 + review fixes) is correct, not arbitrary.
- Optimal-effort smoothness = argument for MoveToward/Lerp blending everywhere
  (already the pattern) over any hard pose snap.
- Symmetry: verify no future pose mirrors both arms identically during a
  dynamic action — one-sided asymmetry is the natural default.
